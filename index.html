The argument. One idea about what Bittensor is, built from the two instruments below it.

Ethereum made Bitcoin's money programmable. Bittensor makes the mining programmable.

Bitcoin's invention was not the coin. It was a way to pay strangers, every block, for work the network defined, with no employer and no contract. Then it defined one job: find a hash.

Ethereum took the invention and opened the money. The mining stayed sealed. Bittensor opens the mining. A subnet is a mining program: what work is wanted, how it is judged, who gets paid. The design space is no longer the layer above the work. It is the work.

01

Bitcoin taught the world one trick.

Pay strangers, every block, for work you define. Bitcoin defined one job, and the job was guessing a number. The hash bought security and nothing else. The mining was never the waste. The fixed puzzle was.

02

Ethereum opened the money.

The design space moved up a layer, to contracts. A decade of chains lives there. Nobody reprogrammed the mine. They reprogrammed what its output could do.

03

Bittensor opens the mining.

The puzzle is a program. A subnet owner writes what the work is, how it is scored, and how the block reward splits. Miners compete on the work. Validators compete on the judgment. The chain does not run the puzzle. It pays the answer.

Verified by re-running, or verified by judging.

Bitcoin's work is trivial to check: "hashes are immediate to validate and everybody can agree" AS 2022. Ethereum's is checked by re-running it. Bittensor's can only be checked by judging it, and the network's own docs call the judgments subjective OTF 2024. So the judging is a paid, competitive market of its own. That is the whole innovation and the whole risk.

On a programmable mine, the bugs are in the incentive.

A hash cannot be gamed. A judgment can. The subnets already write against it: Affine sells a mechanism that is "sybil-proof, decoy-proof, copy-proof, and overfitting-proof" SN120, and Ridges names "applying a stored answer" as hardcoding SN62. Reward hacking is to Bittensor what reentrancy was to early Ethereum: the native exploit of the new design space.

Three fights, one fight.

Research versus revenue, the pace of rule changes, enterprise readiness. Each asks the same thing of a different puzzle: is this work worth paying for before anyone has bought it? The foundation says it plainly: "a subnet nobody uses still collects a slice of every block" OTF 2026. Targon's target is a dollar of revenue for every dollar a miner earns SN4. Bitcoin subsidised security for years before fees mattered. The frame names the bet. It does not settle it.

The instruments

The argument is mine. The instruments are not.

"If you ask five people what Bittensor is, you will get five different answers" BS 2024. That is why the instruments exist. The index measures what a subnet shows a first-time reader. The map records what the people around the network say it is. Both have a method page and a correction form, and neither depends on the argument being right.

Bittensor is a network of small, independent marketplaces for digital work that share one currency. Think of a trade fair with a hundred booths: each booth runs its own competition, pays its own winners, and sells its own product, but every booth is paid out of one shared prize fund. The fund is TAO, a coin created at a steady rate.

A subnet is one booth. It is a set of rules for a single kind of work: miners do the task, validators score it, and pay flows by score. Each subnet has its own token, and since June 2026 its share of the prize fund follows that token's price, so the market decides which booths get funded.

This index asks one question of the 32 best-funded booths: has the subnet told the people who fund it, work for it, buy from it, and encounter it what it sells, in a place they will look, in five minutes? It scores findability, not quality. Every score links to its evidence, and every subnet page opens with a plain-language explainer. Dotted words open a definition.
